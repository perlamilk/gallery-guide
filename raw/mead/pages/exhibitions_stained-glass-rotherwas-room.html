The windows of the Mead Art Museum’s Rotherwas Room are decorated with thirty stained-glass panels produced in various parts of Europe between the late 15th and 18th centuries. As in their original settings, these small compositions are set into larger clear glass windows. Such panels enjoyed broad popularity across social classes and could be found in private homes, churches, town halls, and other public buildings. The examples displayed here encompass a variety of genres, subjects, and techniques, with most featuring coats of arms that echo motifs found in the room’s wall paneling.

Learn More about the Rotherwas Room

One of the signature galleries of the Mead Art Museum, the Rotherwas Room, has many stories to tell. Learn more and listen to audio tours about the room on our Rotherwas Room guide page.

Rotherwas Room Guide

How did these panels come to Amherst College?

While the Rotherwas Room itself was bequeathed to Amherst College by Herbert Lee Pratt (Class of 1895), the stained glass came from the collection of his older brother, George Dupont Pratt (Class of 1893). George D. Pratt originally willed the panels to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, but soon after, the museum introduced a policy of lending objects that could not be displayed in the foreseeable future to smaller institutions. A major factor behind this policy shift was World War II and the museum’s preparations for the emergency evacuation of its collections. Several dozen stained-glass panels were sent to Amherst on permanent loan in 1941. Most were later returned to the Met, while the panels installed in the Rotherwas Room remained on loan. They have been on view since the Mead Art Museum opened in 1950, and formally entered the museum’s collection in 2018.

Where do they originate from?

The majority of panels originate from Germany, but some were produced in Switzerland, Austria, and the Netherlands. Because these panels are small, they were easy for owners to remove and relocate or sell. As a result, they were dispersed into collections around the world, and scholars are rarely able to determine their original locations or full histories of ownership. There are no known records on where George D. Pratt acquired the panels. The fact that most of them have uniform modern clear glass borders with gold floral patterns suggests that they were framed upon entering Pratt’s collection in the early 20th century.

What kinds of spaces were they intended for?

Differing from the windows of Gothic cathedrals that were often entirely made of colored glass and designed to be seen from a distance, these small panels were incorporated into clear glass windows and placed close to the viewer. Such panels were popular not only among the church and nobility but also regular citizens—especially merchants and tradesmen, who by the 16th century had become an important segment of European art clientele. They bought stained-glass panels to decorate their homes or to give as gifts to business partners, newly married couples, churches, town halls, hospitals, schools, inns, and other businesses.

Example of an English interior with stained-glass panels similar to those on view in Rotherwas Room: Edwin Henry Landseer. Georgiana, The Duchess of Bedford, at a Window, ca. 1829-1830. Oil on canvas. Mead Art Museum. AC 1978.112. Museum purchase.

What do they depict?

Image

Caspar Spengler. Heraldic Panel of Hans Jacob Kaffman, 1595. Modern orange fillet border; uncolored glass, vitreous paint, red and blue pot metal glass, silver stain, blue and green enamels. Mead Art Museum. AC 2018.205. Gift of the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

Most of the stained-glass panels in Rotherwas Room display coats of arms, while others contain religious and historical scenes. Such compositions could entertain, offer a moral message, or commemorate a person or an important event. As the panel of Hans Jacob Kaffman demonstrates, some patrons preferred personalized imagery, which not only featured their coat of arms but also referred to their patron saint and occupation. With the medium of printmaking on the rise, some stained-glass panels on religious and historical subjects were adaptations of popular print designs, like this crucifixion scene based on a design by the famous German engraver Martin Schongauer (1450/1451–1493).

What techniques were used here?

The earliest panel in the room, Crucifixion with the Virgin and Saint John the Evangelist, was produced in the traditional medieval technique. In this method, an artist builds the composition out of different-shaped pieces of colored glass, while also adding pieces of clear glass. Both are then painted with special pigments and fired in a kiln. The pieces are soldered together by lead strips, which form a pattern across the glass. Soft oily putty is applied between the glass and the lead to make panels durable and weatherproof. Over the centuries, as cracks appeared, new lead strips could be added to mend the panel.

Deep knowledge of paint pigments and the firing process, skillful brushwork with additional polishing, hatching and scratching to create decorative patterns allowed glass painters to create works as sophisticated as oil paintings.

By the early 17th century, the technology of stained-glass production developed new possibilities. Artists expanded their color palette and started to use larger sheets of glass. They no longer depended upon the use of colored glass or the lead line as an integral part of the design. Instead, images were painted on large clear glass sheets, and the lead served only as a means to hold the sheets together—for example, like in the panel of Jacob de Waerder. The result resembles a painting placed behind a rectangular grid. The sky serves as the background of the image, which constantly changes with the weather and the lighting outside.
