One of the signature galleries of the Mead Art Museum, the Rotherwas Room, has many stories to tell.

Have you ever visited a historic house or room in a museum and left with more questions than answers? We're here to help you find the information you seek about the Rotherwas Room! This beautiful room has been taken apart, reinstalled multiple times, and changed hands and roles. It has survived wars, natural calamities, pest infestations, and a trip across the Atlantic less than a year after the Titanic sank.

Where does the room come from? How was it initially used?

Image

Sir Roger Bodenham. Fragment of a stained glass window in Rotherwas Chapel, ca. 1880s. Courtesy Aidan McRae Thomson

The English knight Sir Roger Bodenham (1545–1623) commissioned this room for his primary residence, Rotherwas Court, in Herefordshire County in England. Although the Bodenhams did not hold a noble title, they had a long history and belonged to the upper class of society. They owned vast lands and multiple mansions in Herefordshire, and prospered from collecting rent from their tenants.

When Sir Roger and his wife Bridget Baskerville came to live at Rotherwas, it was an old timber mansion. The couple added new rooms in stone, including a domestic chapel and the dining room that originally housed these wooden panels. As the most important space in the house, the dining room had the most elaborate decoration—visible proof of the prosperity and high social status of the family. Wood paneling also played a functional role, keeping the room warmer during the winter. A panel to the right of the fireplace marks the completion of the paneling in 1611. This timing must have been connected with the wedding celebrations of October 5, 1611, when Sir Roger’s only daughter Blanch got married in Rotherwas Chapel.

What does the original site look like now?

Image

Aerial photograph showing the parch marks in the grass where Rotherwas House once stood. Rotherwas Chapel can be seen in the upper right corner. Photo by T&T Aerial Photography

Only a footprint of Rotherwas House survives to this day. By the end of the nineteenth century, the Bodenham family fell into financial hardship and could no longer sustain the estate. Put on sale in 1912, the house changed several hands, gradually falling into disrepair until it was torn down completely in 1926. Rotherwas Chapel was set to be demolished too, but survived thanks to the efforts of the local community and is now in the care of English Heritage.

Read more about the Rotherwas Chapel at the Herefordshire County BID

Who designed the room?

There are no records on the designers of the room. Though the style of the carvings reveals a strong Flemish influence, it is more likely that the designers were English and derived the ornamentation from foreign pattern books. As a well-traveled and educated person, Sir Roger must have also been involved in the choice of allegorical figures, heraldic devices and other symbolically charged elements. When the work on the paneling started, Sir Roger was already in his sixties, which suggests that he had retired from active service to the crown and had more time to focus on his estates.

This woodwork must be regarded as an exceptional effort on the part of the owner of Rotherwas, and there is little doubt that designers and craftsmen from South-east of England were imported into Herefordshire for its execution.”
Herbert Cescinsky & Ernest Gribble, Early English Furniture & Woodwork, Vol. 1, London, 1922.

What kind of wood is used here?

Image

View of the Rotherwas Room. Photo by Stephen Petegorsky

Whereas wood paneling of this era was typically done in oak, this kind of wood appears here only in the fireplace mantel and overmantel, and the wall panels are made of walnut. At that time, walnut was not yet available in England domestically and largely came from France. The high cost of walnut led to it being used exclusively for fine furniture and never for wall panels, which makes its use in the Rotherwas dining room exceptional. Like the fireplace, which remarkably retains traces of the original coloration, the walls and the ceiling may have been painted to align with the Jacobean’s era’s love for bright colors and all things curious, ingenious, and excessive.

What does the decoration of the fireplace mean?

Image

Click the above image for a larger detailed view

Fireplace mantel and overmantel. Photo by Stephen Petegorsky

Image

Fragment of the fireplace showing the attributes originally held by Justice and Temperance. Photograph taken at Rotherwas Court ca. 1912. Herefordshire Libraries / Herefordshire History 

The focal point of the room—the grand fireplace—looks like a facade of a classical building. It is embellished with jewel-like ornaments, plants, flowers, and fruit-swags. As a whole, the fireplace displays family ancestry, allegiance, values, and knowledge. Its most notable feature is a large heraldic shield—a proclamation of Sir Roger’s distinguished heritage. The shield is topped by a helmet with a dragon’s head—the Bodenham family crest. 

The human figures on both sides of the shield are caryatids—standing female figures functioning as columns. Here, they personify the cardinal virtues—four principal human virtues in Christian theology. 

From left to right, they represent:

  • Justice (she originally held scales and a sword);
  • Temperance (she carried jugs of water and wine, mixing the two extremes to make a more balanced liquid);
  • Prudence (she holds a snake signifying wisdom, and in her other hand, she may have had a mirror—a symbol of self-reflection);
  • Fortitude (with a column symbolizing her strength and alluding to the columns in the Temple of the Philistines brought down by Samson).

Not only their armor and fierce facial expressions, but the form of the figures makes them look powerful and assertive. The entire construction of the fireplace is a visual metaphor, with the virtues functioning symbolically as the backbone of the family. On the legs of the mantel below, one can also find half-human creatures with goat horns, tails, and legs. 

Those are either ancient Greek satyrs or Roman fauns—mythological creatures associated with fertility, revelry, and the untamed aspects of human nature. Here, they probably served as personifications of bestial instincts tamed and driven to the margins by the triumphant human virtues dominating the room.

What led to the preservation of the room?

English woodworkers of the era used time-tested methods and techniques, which helped ensure the longevity of their work. Panels were precisely joined with mortise and tenon joints. Instead of iron nails, which are prone to corrosion and lead to a faster decomposition of the structure, panels were pinned together with wood pegs (they are visible on certain panels alongside nails, which were added later).

Whereas contemporary wood often comes from younger trees, Renaissance craftsmen used old growth wood, which is denser, harder, and more resistant to decay and pests. Surfaces were skillfully leveled, smoothed, and protected with a finish. Thanks to excellent craftsmanship and owners’ care, the room survived the English Civil War of 1642–1651, the attacks of woodworm that left tunnels in the paneling, a massive fire on the estate in 1907, and multiple transfers from one place to another.

Learn more about the events that placed the room at risk >>

While the quality of craftsmanship was crucial for the preservation of the carvings, there was always a risk that they would be disposed of by a neglectful owner, damaged by insects, or vanish in a fire or flood. That risk increased in times of war and political turmoil. In 1646, during the English Civil War, the Bodenhams had Rotherwas seized and then confiscated by the Parliament because they had remained loyal to King Charles I and, furthermore, were Catholic. In contrast to some Royalist houses that were burned down or looted, Rotherwas survived, but the Bodenhams faced considerable financial difficulties. The change of tenants and owners increased the risk of neglect of the estate. Fortunately, the Bodenhams were able to repurchase Rotherwas as soon as 1653, and over the centuries, the family continued to take care of the paneled interiors. Although the tunnels in many wall panels demonstrate that the woodworm did not bypass the dining room, the attacks—whenever they occurred—were successfully stopped. In 1907, there was a massive fire on the estate which destroyed the extensive stables connected with the mansion. Luckily, the fire was extinguished, and the house with all its paneling survived.

What else contributed to the room's preservation >>

Throughout its existence, except for the times when it was moved, the dining room largely remained in the stable environment of a well-maintained home or a museum. Venerated by its owners, the paneling has been cared for and treated wherever it was installed. Public interest in the room also proved helpful: whereas Rotherwas House was reported to have had thirteen paneled rooms from different periods, only this room—the crown jewel of the house—was ever discussed in detail and remained in the public eye. Its reputation as a magnificent place was amplified by the lore about James I visiting Rotherwas Court. According to the 17th-century historian Thomas Blount, the king was so impressed with the house that he said to his followers: “Non datur cuivis adire Rotherwas” (“Not everyone is permitted to go to Rotherwas”). This ancient Roman proverb originally referred to Corinth—a Greek city that had the reputation of an extremely luxurious and decadent place—and meant that only privileged people could go to Corinth. In the end, the carvings survived not only because of their high-quality craftsmanship and a fortunate chain of historical events, but also because of their status as a family heirloom and a unique work of art.

How has the room changed over time? How was it brought to the United States?

It is said that Pratt acquired the room in 1913 for $350,000, which is the equivalent to more than $11 million in purchasing power today.

Wood paneling of the Jacobean era was made so that it could be transferred from one house to another, just like the furniture, if the owners changed residences. Only the “inner skin”—the decorative wood paneling—was taken out, while the constructive elements of the old room (the floor, the solid walls, and the ceiling) were not moved.

Paneling was then adjusted to fit into a new space with a different plan and proportions. Over its 400-year lifespan, the dining room from Rotherwas House experienced this several times. Here are some of the key events in its history:

  • 1611: The paneling is completed. It decorates a new dining room, which Sir Roger Bodenham has built in stone as an addition to an old fifteenth-century timber house.
  • 1731–1732: The old house is demolished, and the paneling is moved to a new mansion built on the same site by the architect James Gibbs. The panels are rearranged according to the new square-like plan of the room. The room receives a ceiling with oak beams. The initials “CB” are carved (seen to the left of the fireplace)—a reference to Charles-Stonor Bodenham, who commissioned the new building and organized the paneling’s transfer.
  • 1912–1913: The land of the estate is sold at auction in multiple lots. The London- and New York-based art dealer C. J. Charles acquires the paneling of Rotherwas House. He dismantles the dining room, packs it in cases and ships it first to London, then to New York. The news is actively discussed in the press. American journalists claim that the paneled interior will be “one of the most remarkable rooms, artistically and historically” in the United States.
  • 1913: The room is reassembled in Charles’ gallery in New York with some alterations that were meant to reconstruct the original design: more elongated proportions and, instead of oak beams, a new plaster ceiling based on Jacobean designs. Herbert Lee Pratt (1871–1945), an Amherst College graduate and a future president of the Standard Oil Company of New York, acquires the room.
  • 1914: Pratt has the room installed in his new country house “The Braes” on Long Island to add more historical depth to the house and to enhance its association with the old estates of the English nobility. The plan of the room is the same as in Charles’s gallery, but it receives two additional doors on each side of the fireplace.
  • 1944–1949: Following Pratt’s bequest, the room is transferred to Amherst College. A corner of the new Mead Art Museum building is designed specifically to house it. The plan and dimensions of the room, as well as the ceiling, are copied from Pratt’s house, but the chimney-piece is set up on the opposite wall, allowing for more natural light and clearing the view outside the window. The windows are decorated with stained glass panels from different regions and time periods. In the following decades, new details, like outlets, are added to the room.

Red brick mansion built at Rotherwas Court in 1731–1732. Herefordshire Libraries / Herefordshire History  

Image

How has the room been used at the Mead?

When first installed, the space was used by students as a reading room, and Robert Frost organized public readings of his poems here. Later, it became an autonomous gallery space with period-style furniture and decorative arts. Today, the room has multiple functions and is used for exhibitions, museum events, and as a study space for students. Once a private parlor, it is now open and welcoming to all. In response to the uplifting atmosphere of this space, museum security officer Joseph Kosiorek, who enjoys being on duty there, has observed: “It’s a room where you feel like you can do anything.”
